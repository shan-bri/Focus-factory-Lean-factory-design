Breezeway Fans: Focused Factory Simulator

The brief

Breezeway Fans Ltd (a fictional company) makes four models of electric fan: two high-volume domestic models and two low-volume industrial models. Every fan is pressed, machined, wound, assembled, tested and packed; the industrial fans also need dynamic balancing.

The plant runs a push, batch-and-queue system in a process layout: machines are grouped by type, and each model is released once a week as one large batch. Lead times are long, there is a lot of work in progress, and parts travel back and forth across the floor.

Breezeway is moving to a larger site. The managers disagree: one mixed-model line for all four fans, or two focused factories (domestic and industrial)? Your task: design the new factory, choose a batch size, and prove with the simulator which option is better, and under what conditions.

  1. Product families: use visual process-routing analysis to see which models follow the same route, then the capacity check to decide: one family or two?
  2. Build the factory: place machines and tag them to cells. Buy extra machines if a cell needs them. The press cannot be moved.
  3. Simulate: run one month (4 weeks) and compare your design with the current factory.
  4. Improve and debrief: test the alternative, record your runs, and answer the debrief questions.

How the model works

  • The model first runs a 3-week warm-up so the factory is in a normal, steady state, then measures one month (4 weeks of 5 days, 2 shifts × 7.5 h). All results and the playback cover that measured month.
  • Each model is released as a batch every N days of demand (you choose N). Batches queue first-in, first-out at each machine.
  • Availability stretches processing time (e.g. 88% availability means 10 min of work takes about 11.4 min).
  • Changeover: none if the machine last ran the same model; 20% of the full changeover if it last ran a model of the same family (domestic or industrial); otherwise the full changeover.
  • Moves: machines within 10 m (2 tiles) can pass parts by hand (about 2 min). Longer moves need a forklift or pallet truck: about 30 min average wait plus travel.
  • One-piece flow: when consecutive operations are in the same cell and within 10 m, the next machine can start as soon as the first unit arrives.
  • A model uses machines in its own cell first, then shared machines, then machines in other cells (inter-cell moves).

Product families

Assign each model to a family (1–4). Family k will be made in Cell k. One family for all four models means one mixed-model line.

How to read the matrix: models are listed by volume (highest first) and machines in process order. Numbers are cycle times (minutes per unit); a blank means the model does not use that machine.

Visual process-routing analysis: with only a few models and machines, you can find families by looking at the routings. Start with the highest-volume models. Which models follow the same route? Which differ, and how (extra operations, longer cycle times, volume)?

Debrief

Answer with evidence from your run history.

  1. Did you choose one mixed-model line or two focused factories? What numbers convinced you?
  2. Your capacity check: which machine limits each option? What did you buy or move to fix it, and was it worth the money?
  3. Compare weekly, daily and half-day batches. Why does a focused factory cope better with small batches? Use the changeover hours.
  4. Which machines have low utilisation in your design? Is that a problem? Link it to the disadvantages of focused factories from the lecture.
  5. Under +15% demand and late supplier deliveries, which design was more robust? Which countermeasure helped most, and what did it cost?
  6. The press could not be moved. Which form of focused factory does that create for pressing (flow line, workcell or workcenter)?
  7. Name two things this model leaves out that would matter in a real plant (for example operators and skills, quality and rework, maintenance, demand changes).
